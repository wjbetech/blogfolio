If you spend all day at a computer, you'll bump into mechanical keyboards eventually.

What was once a niche hobbyist thing now shows up in offices, home desks, and even cafes. And if you live in Korea, you'll bump into them even faster.

Let's take stock of where I've ended up, and whether those famous Seoul TechnoMart runs are actually worth your afternoon.

## Where the obsession started

Those of us who grew up in the 90s and 00s probably remember the same thing: cheap, rattly Dell or HP boards that came with every school PC and entry level desktop. I've got a weird fondness for them. Some sounded and felt great for what they were, all things considered.

The first mechanical boards felt like the opposite. They were noisy and eccentric, from brands you'd never heard of, with elaborate switch explanations you didn't understand, a wall of LEDs and macro keys, and a price tag that made you wince. That's what made them intriguing.

Mine was a bulky, full-size Turtle Beach. It only did red backlighting, it weighed a ton, and the matte coating wore off faster than I'd have liked. I loved it anyway. It came with a matching red-lit mouse and got me through university, including more World of Warcraft and Old School RuneScape than I'd like to admit. I still think of it fondly, not because it was refined but because it made typing feel like an event.

These days things have shifted. Living in Korea is great for keyboards, but not quite for the reason you'd expect. Most production still happens in China, with a fair bit in Korea too, but the real change is what you get for the money. Switches sound better out of the box, keycaps are thicker, stabilizers don't rattle as much, boards come pre-lubed and already foamed to sound deeper and thockier. I still add a little extra lube myself, but we're a long way from having to do it just to make a board usable.

That progress is kind of why I'm stuck at six boards and finding it harder to justify a seventh.

## The stack on my desk

Here's what I've held onto, and what each one's taught me. We'll keep it short, because the lesson matters more than the spec sheet:

- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>Ducky One 2</span> `TKL` — My reference for a solid, no-nonsense board. It taught me I don't actually need a numpad every day.
- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>Anne Pro 2</span> `60%` — Taught me I love the footprint of a 60% for moving around, but I miss dedicated arrows more than I expected.
- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>RK 60</span> `60%` — The budget surprise. For the price, it punched way above its weight and showed me how far cheap boards have come.
- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>SPM PL87W Mongdol</span> `TKL` — My taste of the local market. It feels a bit like a time capsule of what Korean boards do well: sturdy, a little quirky, and fun to type on.
- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>Magicforce 82 V-2</span> `75%` — Taught me that 75% might be my sweet spot. You keep function keys and arrows without giving up desk space.
- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>Feker Alice</span> `Alice` — The ergonomic experiment. It looks odd at first, but it taught me how much angle and split can matter when you're typing for hours.

None of these is objectively "the best." That's the point. Trying sizes taught me more than reading switch charts ever did. If you're choosing your next board, I'd start with layout first, feel second, and everything else after.

## What Yongsan and Gangbyeon are actually like

I've spent a few afternoons hunting at Yongsan TechnoMart and Gangbyeon TechnoMart, scouting what's on shelves and whether any deal jumps out. If you're picturing floor after floor of custom builds, you'll want to recalibrate.

You'll find a lot of familiar mid-range boards, some office gear, and a rotating cast of gaming-focused stalls. Prices are often fine, but they're rarely better than Coupang or the usual online shops once you compare. Where the marts shine is the scouting: you can see a Mongdol in person, feel a Magicforce's 75% layout, or spot a weird Alice-style board you'd never have clicked on online.

Last time I went, I went in telling myself I was "just looking." I left with nothing, not because there was nothing good, but because I've already got the range at home. That spoilt-for-choice feeling is real. If you've only got one board, you'll learn a lot just by touching a few sizes in person. If you've already got three or four you like, you'll probably walk out thinking you'd rather save the money.

So here's how I'd frame it if you're on the fence:

- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>Go to TechnoMart if:</span> you want to try sizes and layouts with your own hands, you enjoy the treasure hunt, or you want to see what Korean brands like SPM are putting out right now.
- <span className="font-bold" style={{color: "var(--accent-300)"}}>Order online if:</span> you know your size, you've got a switch preference, and you care most about price and selection.

We'll go deeper with photos once I've got the stack shot. For now, let's call the marts great for learning, not great for bargain hunting.

## Why I've (mostly) stopped buying

Modern boards have narrowed the gap so much that another purchase doesn't change my day-to-day as much as it used to. Pre-lubed switches, decent stabilizers, and foam already in the case mean even a budget RK 60 feels pleasant. That's good news if you're buying your first or second board. It's less exciting if you're chasing board number seven.

I don't think I'm done forever. If you've got a spare IBM Model M gathering dust, I'd happily give it a new home. But for now, the most useful thing I've done is figure out what I actually like (a compact 75% or 60% with arrows nearby, something that sounds deep without trying too hard) and resist the rest.

If you're hunting in Seoul this weekend, start with size, borrow a friend's board for an hour if you can, and use TechnoMart to confirm what you think you want. You might find you don't need to buy at all, which is the nicest surprise this hobby can give you.
